Welcome to the Olga Eremina Lab

We build optical tools for biomedical measurement — molecular probes, the assays that carry them into a sample, and the analysis that reads the result. The work sits between analytical chemistry and imaging, and it is pointed at questions in cancer and neurodegeneration that current methods cannot reach.

Probe development starts with chemistry. Reporter molecules are chosen computationally before they are synthesised; plasmonic and fluorescent cores, protective shells, and targeting ligands are optimised together against specificity, stability, and signal. Assays are built the same way — sample handling, acquisition, and machine-learning analysis designed as one system and benchmarked against established methods.

What that buys is breadth per sample: dozens of molecular questions asked of a single tissue section, a microliter of cerebrospinal fluid, or a node found during surgery — without consuming the sample to do it.

The lab opens in January 2027 and is recruiting its first cohort of graduate students, postdocs, and undergraduate researchers now.

One measurement platform, four settings

The same chemistry — a plasmonic core, a Raman reporter, a protective shell, a targeting ligand — is read differently depending on where the sample comes from.

In vitro, the probes screen microliter biofluids non-invasively, picking out rare cells and vesicles from a background that would swamp a conventional assay.

Ex vivo, they turn an archived tissue section into a spatial immune profile — dozens of markers on one slide, with the tissue left intact for proteomics and genomics afterwards.

Intra-operatively, they mark the tissue a surgeon needs to find and then report on its immune composition once it is out.

In vivo, they track vital processes over time without a biopsy, which is how the 26-plex library was first validated in a living animal.

03

The spectral library

Every program depends on the same asset: a set of Raman reporters whose spectra do not overlap. Twenty-six of them exist and have been demonstrated together; the target is sixty-five.

A SERS nanoparticle is a gold core, a monolayer of a small aromatic reporter molecule, and a silica shell that protects it and carries the antibody. The core supplies the field enhancement; the reporter supplies the barcode.

Which reporter to pick is a chemistry question with a computational answer. Calculations predict where a molecule's bands will fall and how strongly it will scatter, so a library can be designed for orthogonality instead of assembled by trial and error. Twenty-six reporters were selected this way, then validated experimentally down to attomolar detection limits.

Getting from twenty-six to sixty-five is not a matter of making more particles. It means co-designing the reporters, the particle architecture, and the acquisition scheme together, so that each new barcode fills a gap in the spectrum rather than crowding a neighbour.

Our research

Why it matters. Multiplexing is limited not by the microscope but by how many distinguishable labels exist. Every additional orthogonal reporter is one more question a single sample can answer — from the same slide, the same microliter, the same animal.

  • Sharp, sparse bandsA reporter with a few strong, narrow peaks occupies less of the spectrum than one with many broad ones, leaving room for its neighbours.
  • Strong enhancementSignal has to survive dilution into a real sample, so predicted enhancement matters as much as band position.
  • Stable adsorptionThe reporter must stay bound to the core through conjugation, storage, and staining without exchanging or desorbing.

Join the lab
