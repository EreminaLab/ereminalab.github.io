Join the lab

Build measurement tools that did not exist last year

In this lab you will synthesise the particle, run the tissue, and write the model that reads the result. Those are usually three different people in three different labs. Here they are one project, and the person who owns it sees the whole chain from a molecule to a number.

No single background is required. Chemists, spectroscopists, materials scientists, and people who enjoy working with data all have a place. What matters more is wanting to know why a measurement behaves the way it does, and being willing to fix it at whichever layer is actually broken.

The lab opens in January 2027. Joining now means helping choose the instruments, writing the first protocols, and setting how it works — more responsibility than a large established one offers, and more credit.

For undergraduates

Undergraduate researchers

Open to UT Austin undergraduates in chemistry, biochemistry, engineering, and related fields, including first- and second-years.

Every undergraduate researcher works toward an independent project and a conference presentation. Several previous mentees have become co-authors.

How to get in touch →

Collaborators. The lab is also interested in hearing from clinicians, pathologists, and neuroscientists with cohorts, samples, or hard measurement problems. If you have a question that needs more channels than your current assay provides, that is exactly the conversation to have.

What to send

Short is fine. Specific is better.

  • One Your CV A PDF. Publications, techniques, and anything you have built or fixed yourself.
  • Two A paragraph on what interests you Which program, and what specifically about it. “I want to work on nanoparticles” tells us nothing; “I want to know why crosslinker length changes aggregation” tells us a great deal.
  • Three Names, for postdoc applications Two or three references. We will not contact anyone without telling you first.

What happens next

Olga reads everything and replies to every message, usually within a week or two. If there is a fit, the next step is a video call — a conversation about the science rather than a formal interview.

For graduate applicants, that conversation is separate from the departmental admissions process, which runs on its own timeline. Talking to the lab early does not replace the application, but it does mean your file is read by someone who already knows who you are.

If you are unsure whether your background fits, ask. The honest answer is that most useful people in this field arrived from an adjacent one.

How the lab works

Worth knowing before you write, so you can decide whether it suits you.

  • You own a project end to endSynthesis, measurement, and analysis stay with one person. It is harder than specialising, and it is the reason people leave here able to start their own thing.
  • Fellowships are part of the trainingEveryone is mentored toward external funding — NSF GRFP, NIH F31/F32, foundation awards. Writing time is planned, not squeezed in.
  • Collaboration is real, not decorativeThe work touches pathology, neurology, and surgical oncology. Expect to sit in rooms with clinicians and to be asked questions your model does not answer yet.
  • Negative results get written downA reporter that does not separate, a conjugation that aggregates — these are findings about the design space, and they save the next person months.
  • Mentoring runs downwardGraduate students mentor undergraduates. It is expected, supported, and counted as work.
  • Everyone presentsLab members present at conferences early, not once they have a paper. Talking about unfinished work is a skill, and it is practised here.
